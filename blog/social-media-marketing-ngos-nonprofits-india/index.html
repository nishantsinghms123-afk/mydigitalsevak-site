Social Media Marketing for NGOs & Nonprofits in India

By Nishant Singh, founder of My Digital Sevak — written after running a social media agency across 63+ brands · Published October 10, 2026

Social media for an Indian NGO works when it proves where the money goes. Post dated field updates, follow up on past appeals, and speak separately to donors (Instagram, WhatsApp), volunteers and CSR partners (LinkedIn). Then stay inside the rules: no identifiable at-risk children, no foreign donations without FCRA, and Meta authorisation for social-issue ads.

An NGO client looks like the easiest account an agency can take on. The stories are real, the cause is good, and nobody argues about whether a reel is "on brand". Then the work starts and it's harder than a restaurant or a clinic. The budget is small, a trustee committee approves every post, the best photos can't legally be used, and the feed has to persuade three groups of people who want completely different things.

This is the playbook we'd hand a new account manager taking on a nonprofit client in India. It applies equally if you're the NGO's own comms person.

Who is an NGO's social media actually for?

The mistake most NGO feeds make is talking to everyone at once, which in practice means talking to the people already inside the organisation. Split the audience first, then decide on platforms:

The CSR row deserves more attention than most agencies give it. Under Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013, any company with a net worth of ₹500 crore, turnover of ₹1,000 crore or net profit of ₹5 crore in the preceding year must spend 2% of its average net profit of the last three years on CSR. The people who allocate that money check an NGO's LinkedIn page before a first meeting. A page with a three-year-old banner and no posts costs the NGO more than any missed Instagram trend. What content builds donor trust?

Indian donors have been burned by WhatsApp forwards about fake causes, so an NGO's feed is read with suspicion first and sympathy second. The content that earns trust is specific and checkable:

  1. Dated, located field updates. "Saturday, 4 October, Kichha block: 62 women attended the anaemia camp" beats a stock photo of smiling children every time.
  2. Follow-ups on earlier appeals. If the NGO raised money for winter blankets in December, post the distribution in January with the count. Almost nobody does this, and it's the single strongest post an NGO can publish.
  3. Real unit costs, from the NGO's own books. "₹1,200 covers one child's school kit for a year" only works if finance has confirmed the number. Never round up for impact.
  4. The paperwork, made visible. Registration type, 12A and 80G status, the latest annual report and audited accounts, linked from the bio. Donors still ask for an "80G receipt", but individuals on the new tax regime can't claim that deduction and cash gifts above ₹2,000 don't qualify. So use tax savings as reassurance, not the headline.
  5. Faces of the team. A two-minute reel from the founder or a field coordinator outperforms polished graphics because it shows that real, accountable people run the organisation.

What can't an NGO post or promote?

This is where an agency earns its fee, because the NGO's own staff often don't know the rules either.

Children's identities

Section 74 of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 bars disclosing the name, address, school or any other detail, including a picture, that could identify a child in need of care and protection, a child in conflict with law, or a child victim or witness. The penalty is up to six months' imprisonment, a fine of up to ₹2 lakh, or both. Only a Child Welfare Committee or Juvenile Justice Board can permit disclosure. Child Welfare Committees have applied this to social media too: in 2024 one in Srinagar acted against Facebook pages that named child victims. The Ministry of Information & Broadcasting has also reminded broadcasters of Section 23 of the POCSO Act, which protects child victims of sexual offences in the same way.

For a shelter, rescue or child-rights NGO, the safe default is: no faces, no names, no school uniforms, no recognisable locations. Shoot hands, backs, silhouettes and activities. They can still look beautiful.

Consent from adult beneficiaries

Get written consent in the person's own language, explain where the photo will appear, and honour withdrawal. A beneficiary's story is personal data, and the DPDP Act guide for agencies covers what consent should look like. Above all, avoid "poverty porn". Show dignity and agency, not helplessness for the sake of clicks.

Foreign donations

An Instagram post with a donate link reaches people abroad. Under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010, an NGO must have FCRA registration (or prior permission) before accepting any foreign contribution, and in January 2025 the Home Ministry warned NGOs of penal action for taking foreign funds without it. If your client isn't FCRA-registered, make sure its payment gateway refuses foreign-source donations before you run a campaign that goes viral among the diaspora.

Do NGO ads need Meta's social-issue authorisation?

Usually, yes. Since December 2021, Meta has required anyone in India running ads about social issues on Facebook and Instagram to verify their identity and location and carry a "Paid for by" disclaimer. The topics include health, education, civil and social rights, environmental politics, crime and the economy, which covers most NGO causes. Ads without authorisation are paused and stay visible in the public Ad Library. If you choose the "Page Name" disclaimer option, the Page must be at least 30 days old.

Practical rule: start authorisation two weeks before any campaign goes live, and complete it on a real admin's account, not a freelancer's.

Google for Nonprofits is worth setting up for any eligible client. It unlocks the YouTube Nonprofit Program (Google reviews applications in 2–14 business days) and, depending on eligibility, Google Ad Grants. Check the current India eligibility criteria on Google's own pages, as they have changed over the years.

A monthly content plan for an NGO client

For a lean retainer, 12–14 feed posts a month plus weekly Stories is enough. Here's the mix we'd start with:

  • 4 field updates, dated and located, at least two as reels.
  • 2 impact posts: a unit cost, a quarterly number, a before-and-after of a facility.
  • 2 volunteer or staff spotlights, shot on a phone, in the person's own words.
  • 1 follow-up on an earlier appeal, with numbers.
  • 1 explainer reel on the cause itself: a government scheme, a health fact, a rights issue.
  • 1 LinkedIn case study written for CSR teams.
  • 1–2 calendar-day posts, only where the cause genuinely fits.

The giving calendar matters more than the cause-day calendar. Plan the year around Daan Utsav (2–8 October), Giving Tuesday in early December, and February–March, when tax-planning donors give before the financial year ends. Then add only the cause days that fit, such as Children's Day on 14 November for an education NGO or World Environment Day on 5 June for a conservation one.

How should an agency price and run NGO work?

We've seen enough agencies take on a "pro bono" NGO that turned into a full-time account with no end date. If you want to give back, give a fixed scope (say, eight posts a month and one campaign a year) and put it in writing like any retainer agreement. Paid NGO work is usually a lean retainer, and two things are worth flagging early:

  • GST is a real cost to them. Your fees carry 18% GST, and an NGO that isn't GST-registered can't claim it back. Quote "₹X + GST" clearly so nobody is surprised at the first invoice.
  • Approvals are slow. Trustees, the founder and sometimes a funder all want to see posts. Batch the month's content, get it approved in one round with a clear deadline, and use a written content approval workflow so nothing goes out on a WhatsApp "ok".
  • Report on outcomes, not likes. Volunteer sign-ups, donation-page visits and CSR enquiries matter more to a board than reach. Build the monthly report around those.

Frequently asked questions

Which social media platform is best for an NGO in India?

Instagram for individual donors and volunteers, LinkedIn for CSR partners, and WhatsApp for the community and existing donors. Most small NGOs should run two of these well rather than five badly. Pick by goal: monthly donors and volunteers point to Instagram; corporate funding points to LinkedIn.

Can an NGO post photos of the children it works with?

Not identifiable ones, where the child is in need of care and protection, in conflict with law, or a victim or witness. Section 74 of the Juvenile Justice Act bars disclosing their identity, including pictures, with up to six months in jail or a ₹2 lakh fine. Shoot hands, backs and activities instead.

Do NGO ads on Facebook and Instagram need special approval?

Often, yes. Since December 2021, ads in India about social issues such as health, education, civil and social rights or the environment need the advertiser to be authorised by Meta and to carry a "Paid for by" disclaimer. Unauthorised ads get paused, so complete authorisation before a campaign launches.

Can an NGO without FCRA registration take donations from abroad through social media?

No. Under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010, an NGO needs FCRA registration or prior permission before accepting any foreign contribution, and the Home Ministry has warned of penal action. A donate link shared on a global platform must refuse foreign-source donations unless the NGO is registered.
