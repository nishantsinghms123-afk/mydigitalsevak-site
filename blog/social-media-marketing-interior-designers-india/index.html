Social media marketing for interior designers in India: sell trust, not mood boards

By Nishant Singh, founder of My Digital Sevak — written after running social media for 63+ brands · Published September 17, 2026

Social media for an interior designer in India should work like a slow, visible portfolio. Show finished homes with budgets and timelines, document projects while they're being built, name your style clearly, and turn Instagram enquiries into paid site visits. Decisions take months, so measure qualified enquiries and signed projects, not likes.

Most of what ranks for this topic is agency service pages: "we make reels, run Meta ads, do SEO — contact us". None of it is wrong, but none of it deals with the things that make interior design different. The ticket size is large, the buyer takes months to decide, the husband, wife and a parent all have a say, and the "product" is someone's private home, which you need permission to show.

This is the playbook we use when an interior studio signs up with us. It works for a solo designer in Jaipur and for a 20-person turnkey firm in Bengaluru, and it's just as useful if you're the designer checking what your agency should be doing.

Why is interior design harder to sell on social media than most categories?

Three reasons, and each one changes what you post.

  • The decision cycle is long. A family buying a flat may start saving interior ideas before the builder even hands over the keys. They will follow three or four studios for months. Your content has to stay useful for that whole time, not just shout offers.
  • The ticket size makes people wary. Nobody hands over lakhs because a reel looked nice. They want to know what it will cost, how long it will take, who will be on site, and what happens when the carpenter disappears for a week.
  • Everyone's feed looks the same. Pull up any ten Indian studio accounts and you'll see the same beige living rooms, the same fluted panels, the same slow pan across a TV unit. If a homeowner can't tell your work from the next studio's, they'll pick on price.

So the content job is less about "look how beautiful" and more about three things: trust, clarity about cost and time, and a recognisable style.

What should an interior designer post?

We build the calendar on five content types. The mix below is our starting point for a studio posting four or five times a week. It's a planning habit, not a statistic — shift it once you see what brings enquiries.

Two rules make this mix work. First, put a budget range on finished projects wherever the client allows it. "3BHK, Gurugram, full home, ₹18–22 lakh, 14 weeks" does more selling than any caption about elegance. Second, pick a style and say it out loud: "warm minimal for Indian joint families" is a positioning; "we do all styles" is not.

Can you show a client's home online?

Only with their clear permission, and that permission should be in writing before the shoot. It's a private home. Family photos on the wall, a child's name on a door, a building that can be identified from the balcony view — all of it tells strangers where someone lives and what they spent. We ask studios to add a line to their design contract covering photography and publication, and to confirm again at handover which rooms and details the client is happy to show. If you're collecting client names and numbers through lead forms, India's data protection law also comes into it; the agency-side view is in our DPDP Act guide for marketing agencies.

A practical tip: many clients who won't allow a full reveal will happily allow a kitchen, a wardrobe detail or a before-and-after of a single wall. Ask for the partial yes.

The "architect" rule designers keep breaking

This one surprises people. Under section 37 of the Architects Act, 1972, only a person or firm registered with the Council of Architecture can use the title and style of "architect". Courts have read this as protecting the title, not the work: an unregistered designer can still design interiors, but can't call themselves an architect. On Instagram you'll see plenty of bios reading "Architect & Interior Designer" and handles with "architects" in them from studios that have no registered architect. Fix the bio, the handle and the ad copy before you start spending on ads.

The rule cuts the other way too. If the studio's founder is a registered architect, the Council's Professional Conduct Regulations, 1989 say an architect shall not advertise their professional services, with listed exceptions — such as their name alongside descriptions of their work in the media (without payment), brochures sent to named potential clients, and listings in directories or on a website. Those rules were written long before Instagram, and I couldn't find the Council spelling out how they apply to social media posts or paid ads. If an architect runs the studio, have that conversation with them before you plan boosted posts under their name. This isn't legal advice.

And the general rules still apply: before-and-after images must be honest (no render passed off as the finished room), and any "complete 2BHK interiors at ₹X" price has to match what the package really includes. Influencer home tours need proper disclosure under ASCI's influencer guidelines.

How do you turn Instagram enquiries into signed projects?

Most studios lose leads here, not in the content. The fix is a short, fixed process:

  1. Qualify in the first reply. City, property type, carpet area, possession date, rough budget. Put these in a saved reply. A family getting keys in eight months is a nurture lead; one with keys next week is a call today.
  2. Move serious leads to WhatsApp or a call within the same day. For the wider question of where client chats should live once someone signs, see WhatsApp vs a client portal.
  3. Charge for the site visit or first consultation, adjustable against the design fee. It filters out people collecting free advice and makes the visit feel valuable.
  4. Keep nurture leads warm with the explainers, not with discount messages. The buyer who saved your "plywood grades" post in March is the one who calls in September.
  5. Record where every signed project came from. Without this, the retainer looks like a cost.

Paid ads help, but only once the organic feed looks trustworthy — people click the ad, land on the profile, and judge the last nine posts. Target by city and by new-possession areas rather than broad "home decor" interests, which pull in people who want inspiration, not a contractor.

What should the monthly report show?

A studio owner cares about projects, not reach. Report on:

  • Enquiries received, and how many met the qualifying criteria
  • Site visits or paid consultations booked
  • Projects signed where the client first found the studio on social, with the project value
  • Which posts were saved and shared most — this tells you which explainers to make next
  • Nurture leads by expected possession month

Because one signed home can be worth more than a year of retainer, a studio can justify a higher fee than most local clients — the ranges we see are in how much to charge for social media management in India. The full report structure is in monthly social media reports for clients.

The 30-second version

  • Treat social media as a slow portfolio for a buyer who decides over months.
  • Post finished homes with city, area, budget range and timeline; show sites in progress.
  • Pick a named style — "all styles" makes you compete on price.
  • Get written permission before photographing any client's home.
  • Don't use "architect" unless someone is registered with the Council of Architecture.
  • Qualify every enquiry, charge for site visits, and report signed projects.

FAQ

Which platform works best for interior designers in India?

Instagram for proof and enquiries, Pinterest for being found while homeowners collect ideas, and YouTube for long walkthroughs that pre-sell a high budget. Most Indian studios should run Instagram well before adding anything else, and use WhatsApp only for enquiries that have already shown intent, never for cold broadcasts.

Can an interior designer call themselves an architect on Instagram?

Only if they are registered with the Council of Architecture. Section 37 of the Architects Act, 1972 reserves the title and style of architect for registered architects and firms. Unregistered designers can still do design work, so write interior designer or design studio in the bio, handle and ad copy.

How long does social media take to bring interior design clients?

Longer than most categories, because a homeowner often follows a studio for months before possession or renovation. Expect the first real enquiries in two to three months and signed projects later. Judge the retainer on qualified site-visit requests and signed projects over six to twelve months, not monthly follower counts.
