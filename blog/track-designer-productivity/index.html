How to Track Designer Productivity Without Killing Creativity

By Nishant Singh, founder of My Digital Sevak — written after running a social media agency across 63+ brands · Published 25 Nov 2026

Track designer productivity by what reaches the client, not by hours or screen activity. Five numbers are enough: the share of work handed in by its due date, the share approved without being sent back, how much of the month's assigned work got finished, attendance, and a manager's rating for quality. Review them monthly, with the reasons behind them.

Every agency owner has had this week. Clients are chasing, three posts are late, and you look across at your designers and wonder: are they slow, or am I giving them too much? Then you search for "how to track designer productivity" and land on time-tracking and monitoring tools, all promising to show you exactly where every minute went.

I run a social media agency with designers and video editors, and I've tried the minute-by-minute route. It told me a lot about minutes and nothing about whether the client was happy. What actually works for a creative team is simpler, fairer and harder to game: measure the work as it reaches the client.

Why are hours and screen activity the wrong measure for designers?

Because the most valuable part of a designer's day doesn't look like work. Finding the idea for a festival post, scrolling references, sketching three layouts and throwing two away: activity software reads that as idle time. Meanwhile, a designer resizing the same banner eight times looks very busy.

Even vendors who sell time trackers say this. Paymo's guide on measuring creative productivity calls "measured solely by output volume" a myth, and notes that hours worked or time per task "fail to capture the essence of creative efforts".

There's a second problem. Much of a designer's day isn't design at all. Asana's Anatomy of Work research has repeatedly found that people spend around 60% of their time on "work about work": chasing status, searching for files, switching apps. In an agency, that's the designer waiting for the client's logo, hunting for last month's brand fonts on WhatsApp, or asking which caption is final. If you measure hours, you're partly measuring how disorganised your process is.

If a designer is slow because the brief arrived late and the assets were scattered, a productivity score that blames the designer is measuring you.

What should you measure instead?

Five numbers, worked out per person per month. Here's how we define them:

NumberHow to work it outWhat it tells youWhat it misses
On-time handoverWork handed in by the end of its due day ÷ work due that monthReliability, the thing clients feel mostWhether the due date was realistic
First-time approvalFinished posts never sent back for changes ÷ finished postsQuality and understanding of the briefClients who send everything back by habit
Work finishedAssigned work completed ÷ assigned workWhether the load is getting doneA carousel is harder than a quote card
AttendanceDays checked in ÷ working days (planned leave and holidays excluded)Discipline and availabilityEverything about the work itself
Manager rating1–5 stars for the month, with a one-line reasonTaste, initiative, attitude, the things numbers can't seeCan be biased, so keep it to one part of the score

Two details matter more than they look. First, "handed in" means the day the designer submitted the work for review, not the day it was posted. If a post is made on Monday for a Friday posting date, the designer did their job on Monday. Mixing up the making day and the posting day is the most common way agencies punish designers for the client's slow approval. We go deeper on this in task management for a social media agency.

Second, only count work whose due date has passed. Otherwise the 3rd of the month looks like a disaster for everyone.

How much should each number count?

For designers and editors, we weight it like this, out of 100:

  • On-time handover: 30
  • First-time approval: 25
  • Manager rating: 20
  • Work finished: 15
  • Attendance: 10

Reliability and quality carry more than half the score because those are what keep clients. Raw volume is deliberately low: weight it heavily and you'll get fast, forgettable posts. If a number has no data for someone (say, a part-timer who doesn't mark attendance), drop it and re-weight the rest rather than giving them zero.

How do you compare a reel editor with a static designer?

You don't compare them on raw counts. One person making 40 quote posts a month and another making 12 reels are both doing a full job. Two fixes:

  1. Use shares, not totals. All five numbers above are percentages of that person's own assigned work, so a reel editor isn't punished for making fewer pieces.
  2. Plan load in work units. When you assign work, count a static as 1, a carousel as 2–3, and a reel as 3–5 depending on the edit. Agree a fair monthly load in units with each person. This is a planning tool, not a score: it stops you overloading someone in the first place.

And separate the designer's revisions from the client's. A send-back because the logo was wrong is on the designer. A send-back because the client changed the offer after approving the plan is not. When you review first-time approval, look at the reasons. Our guide to handling client revisions covers how to cap the second kind.

A monthly review that doesn't feel like surveillance

Numbers only help if the team trusts them. This is the routine we follow:

  1. Tell the team what's measured before the month starts. The five numbers, the weights, and nothing hidden.
  2. Each person sees only their own card. No public league table. A designer and an account manager do different work, and ranking colleagues turns them into rivals.
  3. Run a trial month first. Show scores with nothing attached, so people can see the system is fair before it matters.
  4. Read the lowest number with the person. Low on-time is often a status habit (finished work never marked as handed in), not slow work.
  5. Fix one thing per month. Brief quality, asset folders, or deadline reminders. Not everything at once.
  6. Reward small and reliably. If you tie money to it, keep it modest and predictable. We explain our setup in the employee rewards program guide.

Frequently asked questions

How do you measure a graphic designer's productivity?

Measure what the client receives, not the hours. Track the share of assigned work handed in by its due date, the share approved without being sent back, how much of the month's work got finished, and attendance, then add a manager's rating for quality. Review the numbers monthly, alongside the reasons behind them.

How many posts should a social media designer make in a day?

There is no fair single number, because a simple quote card and an eight-slide carousel are different jobs. Agree a daily load in work units with each designer, weighting carousels and reels higher than statics, and judge them on finishing what was agreed on time rather than on a raw post count.

Should I use time tracking or screen monitoring for designers?

Use time tracking if you bill clients by the hour or need to price jobs, because it tells you what work costs. Avoid screen monitoring for creative staff. Thinking, sketching and searching references look idle to activity software, and monitoring tends to lower trust and effort rather than raise them.
