Social Media Crisis Management Plan for Brands in India: The One-Page Plan to Agree Before Anything Goes Wrong

By Nishant Singh, founder of My Digital Sevak — written after running a social media agency across 63+ brands · Published 24 Nov 2026

A social media crisis management plan is a one-page agreement between a brand and its agency, written before anything goes wrong. It sets severity levels that separate a complaint from a crisis, names a decision-maker and a backup, holds pre-approved statements in English and the local language, and says who pauses every scheduled post and ad.

Every brand crisis we've handled started the same way: a screenshot in a WhatsApp group, sent by someone who wasn't the client, at a time when nobody was at a desk. The question in the first ten minutes is never "what should we say?" It's "who is allowed to decide what we say?" If that answer isn't written down, the agency waits, the founder is on a flight, and the brand's next scheduled post, a cheerful offer graphic, goes live into the middle of it.

Big brands have PR firms for this. The restaurants, clinics, schools and D2C brands most Indian agencies work with don't. So the plan has to be small enough that a founder will actually read it during onboarding, and specific enough that your most junior community manager can follow it on a Sunday night.

What counts as a social media crisis, and what is just a complaint?

Most negative comments are not crises, and treating them like one does damage of its own. Brandwatch puts it plainly in its crisis guide: "Not every uptick in negative feedback signals a media crisis," and a knee-jerk public apology can draw attention to a non-issue. Its red flags are a conversation that is growing fast, hostility that is intensifying, calls for a boycott, and accounts with large followings joining in.

We turn that into three levels, agreed with every client at onboarding:

LevelWhat it looks likeWho owns itResponse timeWhat pauses
1. ComplaintOne customer, one problem: late delivery, cold food, a rude callAgency community managerSame working dayNothing
2. IssueSeveral people saying the same thing, a local influencer or journalist posting about it, or one post being widely sharedAgency account lead + client's named contactWithin 2 hours, any dayPromotional posts about the affected product or branch
3. CrisisA boycott hashtag, media calls, anything touching religion, safety, health, a legal claim, or staff being targetedClient's decision-maker; agency executesHolding statement within 1 hourEvery scheduled post and every running ad, on every platform

The useful thing about levels is that they settle the argument in advance. A community manager doesn't need anyone's permission to call a Level 1. Anyone on either team can call a Level 3, and nobody gets blamed for calling one that turns out to be a Level 2.

What goes into the one-page plan?

This is the template we fill in for every client during onboarding. It fits on one page, and it lives somewhere both teams can open from a phone.

  1. Contact sheet. The client's decision-maker and a backup, the agency lead and a backup. Two phone numbers each, and a note on who is reachable on Sundays, festivals and family weddings.
  2. The three severity levels above, with examples written for this client's business.
  3. Who approves public words. For Level 3, only the decision-maker. The agency never posts an apology, a refund promise or a statement about staff without written approval.
  4. Two or three holding statements, approved in advance, in English and the language the brand's customers comment in.
  5. The kill switch. A list of everywhere content is queued (your scheduler, Meta Business Suite, YouTube, the ads account) and who pauses each one.
  6. Comment rules. What gets hidden (abuse, threats, phone numbers and addresses, spam) and what stays visible (criticism, however harsh).
  7. Account access. Who has admin on each page, where two-factor codes go, and the recovery steps. A hacked page posting fake giveaways is a crisis too.
  8. A log. Screenshot and timestamp every key comment, reply and decision as it happens.
  9. A review date, within a week of any Level 2 or 3.

If your agency keeps written SOPs, this becomes one of them, with a copy per client.

Which crises do Indian brands actually face?

In our experience, the crises that hit small and mid-sized Indian brands are rarely national news. They are:

  • A service complaint that travels. A video of a hair in the biryani, a school fee dispute, a clinic's billing. It spreads through local Facebook groups and WhatsApp forwards before it reaches the brand's own page.
  • A festival or religious post that lands badly. The wrong deity, a joke about a community, a sale post on a day of mourning. Festival calendars are where Indian brands take the most risk. Our festival marketing calendar lists the dates to plan around.
  • Impersonation. Fake pages using the brand's name and logo to run "lucky draw" scams. Customers blame the real brand.
  • Bad timing. A cheerful scheduled post going live hours after a local tragedy or a national event. Brandwatch notes that common blunders often involve forgetting to pause scheduled posts.
  • Staff in the story. An employee's video, or employees themselves being targeted.

The last one deserves a word. When Tanishq withdrew its "Ekatvam" ad in October 2020 after the #BoycottTanishq backlash, the reason it gave was the well-being of its employees, partners and store staff. People still argue about whether withdrawing was right, and that is the point: it was a business decision only the company could make, about the safety of people in its stores. No agency should be making that call. A good plan makes sure the person who should make it is reachable within the hour.

The first 60 minutes of a Level 3, step by step

  1. Minute 0–5: Stop the feed. Pause every scheduled post and every running ad. This is the one step the agency takes without asking.
  2. Minute 5–15: Call, don't message. Phone the client's decision-maker. If there's no answer in ten minutes, phone the backup. Post the screenshot in your internal group, not the client's group with fifteen people in it.
  3. Minute 15–30: Get the facts from the client. What actually happened? Is anyone hurt? Is it true? Don't draft anything clever until you know.
  4. Minute 30–45: Post the holding statement once the decision-maker says go, on the platform where the conversation is happening, not on every platform at once.
  5. Minute 45–60: Reply consistently. Use one agreed short reply under the loudest comments, with a phone number or email that reaches a real person. Start the log.

A holding statement buys time without admitting anything that isn't yet known. Two we keep on file:

"We've seen the posts about [issue] and we're taking it seriously. We're looking into what happened and will share an update by [time] today. If you were affected, please write to [email] or call [number] so we can speak to you directly."
"[issue] ke baare mein aapke messages humne dekhe hain. Hum ise gambhirta se le rahe hain aur aaj [time] tak update denge. Agar aap is se prabhavit hue hain, to [number] par humse seedhe baat karein."

Notice what neither says: no blame, no "we apologise for any inconvenience," no promise the brand might not keep.

Should you delete negative comments?

Hide abuse, threats, slurs, spam and anyone's personal details. Leave genuine criticism up and reply to it. Brandwatch warns that turning off comments can make things worse, and in India the reason is obvious: people who can't comment on your page post the screenshot in five WhatsApp groups instead, where you can't reply at all.

Don't argue in the comments, don't reply to every person with a different story, and never let the client's cousin reply from his personal account to "defend the brand."

What happens after the crisis?

Hold a 30-minute review within a week. What triggered it, how long each step took, which posts went live when they shouldn't have, and what changes. Then restart the calendar slowly: the first posts back should be useful or quiet, not a discount. Mention the review in the monthly report so the client sees the work that went into a bad week.

Nobody plans for a crisis because they expect one. It's preparation: KPMG India, citing industry surveys, notes that 95% of leaders say their social media crisis plan needs improvement. Whether your clients are restaurants in Lucknow or clinics in Bengaluru, one page signed at onboarding is cheaper than one bad Sunday.

Frequently asked questions

Should an agency post an apology on the client's behalf?

Never without written approval from the client's named decision-maker. The agency can post a pre-approved holding statement on its own authority, because the client signed off on that wording in advance. An apology admits something, and only the business owner, sometimes with a lawyer, can decide what the brand admits.

Is it okay to turn off comments during a social media crisis?

Usually not. Turning comments off reads as hiding, and people move the conversation to their own posts and WhatsApp groups where you cannot reply at all. Hide abuse, threats, spam and anyone's personal details, keep genuine criticism visible, and reply to it with the same calm statement and a way to reach a real person.

Who should own the social media crisis plan, the brand or the agency?

Both sign it, but they own different parts. The agency owns monitoring, the severity call at Level 1 and 2, pausing posts and executing replies. The brand owns the decisions: what the business admits, refunds, withdraws or says to the press. Write both names next to every step so nobody waits for permission at midnight.
