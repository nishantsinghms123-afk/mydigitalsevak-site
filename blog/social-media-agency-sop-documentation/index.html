Social Media Agency SOPs: The Five That Actually Matter

By Nishant Singh, founder of My Digital Sevak — written after running a social media agency across 63+ brands · Published September 20, 2026

An SOP is a one-page document that lets someone else do a job to your standard without asking you. A social media team needs five: the month cycle, account access and offboarding, pre-publish checks, escalation, and a per-client handover. Each gets one owner and a review date.

Here is the only test of your documentation that matters. Your best manager tells you on Monday that she is leaving. How many days until a client notices?

If the honest answer is "about three", you do not have an agency, you have a group of freelancers with a shared logo. That was us for the first two years, and it only became visible when someone left in the middle of Diwali campaign season and took four client relationships' worth of context with her — the tone rules, the WhatsApp number for approvals, which cousin of the owner has veto power on creatives. None of it was written anywhere.

What follows is not a documentation project. It is five pages.

Why does a five-person agency need SOPs at all?

Because the maths of Indian agency staffing guarantees you will lose people. Storyboard18 reported in November 2025 that India's ad-agency workforce stands at just over 6.5 lakh with attrition running around 10–12%, and that most hiring is replacement-driven rather than growth-driven. Apply that to a six-person delivery team and you are re-staffing roughly one seat every eighteen months. Every one of those transitions is either a two-week handover or a three-month wound.

There is also a quieter reason. Undocumented process is what stops you raising the number of clients each person can hold — the coordination tax rises with every new brand because nothing is standard. Our piece on how many clients one manager can handle makes the case in numbers; SOPs are the lever that shifts the ceiling.

An SOP is not a policy, a brand guideline or a training manual. A policy says what is allowed. An SOP says what to do, in order, with the screens named. If your document contains the word "ensure" more than twice, it is a policy pretending to be an SOP.

1. The month cycle

One page, calendar-shaped, with dates and owners against each step: brief and plan by the 22nd, internal review by the 24th, calendar to client by the 25th, client approval window of three working days, production, scheduling, publish, report by the 5th. The approval stages themselves are a separate design decision — our content approval workflow template covers the four stages and who signs off at each — but the SOP is the version with real dates and real names attached.

The two lines people skip and shouldn't: what happens when the client misses the approval window (we publish the approved subset and flag the gap), and what happens when a festival shifts the calendar. In India the second one is not an edge case — Diwali, Eid, Onam, Durga Puja and the wedding season reshape half the year's plans, and "we'll figure it out" is how a team of four ends up working a Sunday.

2. Account access and offboarding

This is the SOP with actual legal weight now. It lists, per client, which assets exist, who holds admin, which agency staff have access, and the exact sequence on someone's last working day — remove from the business portfolio, revoke the scheduler seat, rotate anything shared, transfer ownership of files, confirm in writing to the client.

Two things make this urgent rather than tidy. First, you should not be holding client passwords at all; the access model that avoids it is in our client onboarding guide. Second, the DPDP Rules, 2025 were notified on 14 November 2025, fully operationalising the Digital Personal Data Protection Act with a phased compliance runway — and an agency that processes a client's customer data on their instructions sits squarely inside that framework. What was a housekeeping habit is now a compliance artifact. We wrote up the agency-specific obligations in what the DPDP Act means for marketing agencies; the SOP is simply the operational half of it.

3. Pre-publish checks

A checklist, not prose, run by whoever schedules. Ours is eight lines and takes under a minute:

  • Right client account, right handle — the single most common live error in a multi-client agency
  • Caption proofread; brand name and product spelling verified against the brand kit
  • Every tag and mention checked to be the real account, not a fan page
  • Link in bio or sticker updated if the post refers to one
  • Paid partnership or advertisement disclosure applied where required
  • Music cleared for a business account — trending audio is usually not
  • Scheduled time in IST, and not during a national mourning or crisis window
  • Approval on record against this specific version, not a previous one

Two of those deserve their own reading: disclosure rules are covered in our notes on the ASCI influencer guidelines, and the audio question in why brand accounts cannot use trending music.

4. Escalation and client communication

Who replies to comments and DMs, in what window, in whose voice, and the three categories that stop being the manager's decision: a complaint alleging harm or a product defect, anything political or religious, and any mention of legal action or a regulator. Those go to the founder within the hour, unanswered.

Also write down the boring half — which channel is official. If approvals happen on WhatsApp, on a personal number, owned by one person's phone, then that person is your single point of failure no matter how good your other four SOPs are. That is the specific failure the WhatsApp versus client portal comparison was written about.

5. The per-client handover note

One page per client, updated whenever something changes, written for a competent stranger. Brand voice in three lines and two examples of what the client has rejected before. Who actually approves, and who can veto. Payment terms and GSTIN. Where the assets live. The last three months' performance in one line each. Landmines — the topics, colours or competitors that cause a problem. And one sentence on why they hired you, which is the thing most likely to be forgotten and most likely to explain everything else.

When someone resigns, this page is what survives the notice period. It is also, incidentally, the fastest way to spot an account drifting towards the exit: if nobody can fill in "why they hired us", that is a churn signal, and our piece on reducing client churn explains why it usually shows up months before the client says anything.

How do you write these without it becoming a project?

Never write an SOP from memory at a desk. Write it while doing the work.

  1. Record, don't draft. The person who does the job screen-records themselves doing it once, narrating in whatever language they think in. Five to ten minutes.
  2. Turn it into steps the same day, while it is fresh. Numbered actions, screen names, no adjectives.
  3. Have a different person follow it without asking questions. Every place they get stuck is a missing step, and you will average three per document.
  4. Put an owner and a date at the top. "Owner: Priya · Last reviewed: 20 Sep 2026." Unowned documents rot silently.
  5. Stop at one page. If it does not fit, it is two SOPs.

Five documents, one afternoon each, done in a week if you actually schedule it. The founder should edit, not author — founder-written SOPs describe how the founder wishes the work happened, which is why nobody follows them.

How do you stop SOPs from rotting?

Two rules have kept ours alive. The SOP is wrong, not the person. When someone deviates and the outcome was fine, the document gets updated rather than the person corrected — otherwise people quietly stop reading it. And every repeated mistake ends in a line, not a lecture: the same error twice means one sentence added to the relevant checklist, and that is the entire post-mortem.

Review all five when something structural changes — a new hire, a new platform, a new statutory obligation. Two landed in the last year alone: the mandatory appointment letter for every worker under the labour codes, and the DPDP Rules above. Both belong in an SOP, not in your memory.

Where they live matters as much as what they say. A folder of PDFs nobody opens is decoration. Attach the pre-publish checklist to the scheduling task, pin the month cycle inside the calendar the team already works in, and keep the handover note on the client record. Whether you are a three-person shop in Dehradun or a fifteen-person team in Jaipur, the SOP that gets followed is the one that appears at the moment the work happens — and it makes the next monthly report and the next revision round cost you a great deal less than they do today.

Frequently asked questions

How many SOPs does a small social media agency need?

Five is enough to start: the month cycle, account access and offboarding, pre-publish quality checks, escalation, and a per-client handover note. Anything beyond those is usually written to feel organised rather than to be used. Add a sixth only when a specific mistake has repeated twice.

How long should an SOP be?

One page, or one screen. If it does not fit, you are documenting two different procedures and should split them. Every SOP needs a named owner and a last-reviewed date at the top, because an undated document nobody owns is how teams end up following instructions that stopped being true.

Where should social media SOPs live?

Wherever the work happens, not in a separate folder. An SOP stored in a drive nobody opens is decoration. Attach the checklist to the task, pin the month cycle inside the calendar, and keep the handover note on the client record so it is read at the moment it is needed.

Who should write the SOPs — the founder or the team?

The person currently doing the job writes the draft; the founder edits it once. Founder-written SOPs describe how the founder wishes things worked. The fastest method is to have someone narrate the task while doing it, record the screen, and turn that recording into a page the same day.
