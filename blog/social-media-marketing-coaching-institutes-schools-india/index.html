Social media marketing for coaching institutes and schools in India

By Nishant Singh, founder of My Digital Sevak — after running content for 63+ brands · Published September 3, 2026

Education clients are won on admission-season timing and lost on result-day posts. Since November 2024, India's CCPA guidelines make the topper collage most coaching centres publish a regulated advertisement: selection claims need substantiation, and a candidate's name, photo or testimonial needs written consent taken after their selection.

Every agency wants an education client. The retainers are decent, the content brief writes itself, and the client has a genuine seasonal urgency that makes them easy to sell to. Then April arrives, the results come out, and the client sends you 40 photographs of smiling students and asks for a "150+ selections" creative by 6 PM.

That creative is the single most common way an Indian agency lands its education client — and its education client's promoter — in front of a regulator. So let's do the rules first, and the growth playbook second.

What rules apply to coaching and school advertising in India?

On 13 November 2024, the Central Consumer Protection Authority notified the Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisement in Coaching Sector, 2024. They are not a code of conduct. They are enforceable through the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, and they were written specifically because of the creatives your client is asking you to make.

The scope is broad. As Cyril Amarchand's summary sets out, "coaching" covers academic support, guidance, instruction, study programmes or tuition involving more than fifty students, and expressly excludes counselling, sports, dance, theatre and other creative activities. If your client runs an IIT-JEE institute, a spoken-English centre, a CA test series or a school with a competitive-exam wing, they are inside it. A Bharatanatyam academy is not.

Four rules do almost all the work for a social media team:

  • Selection and success claims must be substantiable. Exaggerated or false claims about the number of selections, ranks, success rates, guaranteed selection, job security or salary increases are prohibited outright.
  • Written consent, taken after selection. A successful candidate's name, photograph, testimonial or video cannot be used without their written consent, and that consent must be obtained after the selection — not bundled into the admission form in June.
  • Full context, same prominence. Where a successful candidate is shown, the important information — rank, name, and the duration of the course they actually took — must be displayed prominently. Disclaimers cannot be in a smaller font than the claim. The 8pt asterisk line at the bottom of the hoarding is now a compliance failure by design.
  • No manufactured scarcity. Creating a false sense of urgency or scarcity to push a decision is named as a prohibited practice. "Only 4 seats left" is the education industry's favourite line and is now a risk.

The penalties are real. Under Section 21 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, the CCPA can impose up to ₹10 lakh for a first contravention and up to ₹50 lakh for subsequent ones, order the ad discontinued, and bar an endorser from endorsing for a year — three years for repeat offences.

What does an enforcement case actually look like?

This is where the guidelines stop being theoretical. The CCPA has been enforcing hard against exactly the claim your client wants on a Reel.

In October 2025 the CCPA fined Drishti IAS ₹5 lakh over its "216+ selections in UPSC CSE 2022" claim. The investigation found that 162 of those 216 candidates — about 75% — had only attended the institute's free Interview Guidance Programme, after independently clearing Prelims and Mains on their own. It was the institute's second penalty; a ₹3 lakh fine had already been imposed in September 2024 over a "150+ selections" claim for UPSC CSE 2021, where 148 of 161 candidates were again only free-IGP attendees. Vajiram and Ravi was penalised ₹7 lakh on similar grounds. Across the sector, the CCPA has issued 54 notices and imposed over ₹90.6 lakh in penalties on 26 coaching institutes.

Read the Drishti finding carefully, because it contains the operating rule for your creative team: a selection is only yours if the course actually produced it. A free crash course, a two-week interview module or a test series a candidate bought after clearing Mains does not make that rank your rank. The moment your caption implies otherwise, the caption is the offence — and it went out under your agency's login.

A practical filter for result week: for every face on the creative, your client must be able to answer "which paid course, for how long?" in one line. If they can't, that face doesn't go on the creative. Get the answer in writing before you design, not after the client's lawyer calls.

The result-day post, rewritten

Nobody wants to hear "don't post results" — results are the product proof in this category. The fix is not silence, it's specificity. Here is the same content, made defensible.

What most institutes postWhat the same post should say

Grid of 40 topper faces, no contextEach face with name, rank and the course + duration taken — same font size as the headline

The right-hand column is not just safer. In my experience it converts better, because a parent comparing three institutes on WhatsApp is doing exactly this arithmetic anyway — and the institute that shows the denominator is the one that reads as honest.

When does admission season actually start?

Agencies plan education campaigns around results. Parents decide before results. That gap is where most education retainers underperform.

The volumes make the seasonality obvious — 22,79,743 candidates registered for NEET UG 2026, and JEE Main 2026 recorded its highest-ever registrations. But that is the exam calendar, not the buying calendar. The buying calendar runs about six weeks ahead of it:

WindowWhat's happeningWhat you should be running
Feb – MarBoards on, parents quietly shortlisting for the April batchFaculty credibility content, fee transparency, campus walkthroughs
Apr – JunResults out, peak enrolment, peak ad competitionCompliant result posts, batch-start dates, counselling-call ads
Jul – SepDead zone everyone abandonsCheapest reach of the year: doubt-solving, study-technique, parent content
Oct – NovSecond intake, droppers and repeaters decidingDropper-batch positioning, "restart" messaging, alumni stories
Dec – JanBoard practicals, exam-stress peakRevision content, parent-anxiety content, next-year early-bird

The July–September window is the one I'd fight the client for. Everyone's budget goes to April and October, CPMs spike, and the institute that has been posting useful content all monsoon is the one already saved in a parent's phone when the shortlisting starts. Education is the clearest niche I know where the off-season is the actual advantage.

Who are you actually writing to — the student or the parent?

Both, and they are not on the same platform, in the same language, or in the same mood. Getting this split wrong is why so much education content looks busy and converts nothing.

  • Class 6–10 and school admissions: the parent decides, fully. Facebook and WhatsApp still outperform Instagram here, especially in tier-2 and tier-3 towns. What moves them: safety, faculty stability, transport, fee structure, and other parents visibly choosing you.
  • Class 11–12 and entrance coaching: the student shortlists, the parent approves. You need two content lines running in parallel — Reels and doubt-solving for the student's feed, and results, fees and faculty credentials for the parent's WhatsApp forward. The student brings you three names; the parent picks one.
  • Droppers, repeaters and adult upskilling: the student decides alone. Instagram and YouTube, and the tone shifts completely — this audience has already failed once and is allergic to hype. Honest content about what went wrong and what changes wins here.

Language matters more than production budget, as in most Indian niches. A Patna physics faculty explaining rotational motion in Hindi on a phone camera will beat a ₹40,000 animated explainer for the same institute. If you're running education accounts in a market like Patna or Indore, treat the local language as the default and English as the exception.

The content that actually fills batches

  1. Faculty-led teaching clips, 45–90 seconds. The single highest-return format in this category. Parents are buying a teacher, not a building. One concept, one whiteboard, one take.
  2. Answer the doubts that come to the front desk. Fee structure, batch timings, hostel, transport, "is the dropper batch worth it" — the admissions counsellor already knows the top twenty questions. That's your quarter's calendar, and it's pre-validated by real buyers.
  3. Alumni, done properly. Not a topper collage. One student, one story, one video, consent on file, course and duration named. One of these outperforms a grid of forty faces.
  4. Google Business Profile is the conversion engine. "JEE coaching near me" is where the intent actually lives. Accurate timings, photos, a working call button, real reviews.
  5. WhatsApp closes it. A bio link with a pre-filled "I want batch details for Class 11 JEE" message beats any lead form. Just keep broadcasts factual — the CCPA's urgency rule applies to a WhatsApp blast as much as to a hoarding.

How do agencies run education clients without a compliance incident?

The same way we handle healthcare clients: the risk is not in the strategy, it's in the one post that goes out at 9 PM on result day when everyone is excited and nobody is checking.

Three things I'd insist on in any education retainer:

  • A consent register, maintained by the client, referenced by you. One row per student whose face you may use: name, rank, course, duration, consent date, consent document. If a student isn't in the register, they don't go in a creative. This is the client's legal obligation, but you are the one who will publish the breach.
  • A four-check claims review before anything goes to the client. Is every number substantiated? Is every face consented? Is the course-and-duration disclosure as prominent as the claim? Is there any urgency language? Thirty seconds, applied by the writer.
  • Approval-gated publishing with a timestamp. Result week is chaos — the institute's director is approving creatives between counselling calls. You need a record of exactly which caption and which creative they approved, not a WhatsApp thread you can't search by Monday. That's the whole argument for moving approvals out of chat.

Frequently asked questions

Can coaching institutes advertise their toppers on social media in India?

Only with written consent taken after the candidate's selection, and only alongside the rank, the candidate's name and the duration of the course they actually took. The CCPA's Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisement in Coaching Sector, 2024 require that disclosure to appear as prominently as the claim itself.

What is the penalty for a misleading coaching advertisement?

Under Section 21 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 the CCPA can impose a penalty of up to ₹10 lakh for a first contravention and up to ₹50 lakh for subsequent ones, order the advertisement discontinued, and bar an endorser for up to one year, extending to three years for repeat offences.

Do the CCPA coaching guidelines apply to schools and small tuition centres?

The guidelines define coaching to cover academic support, guidance, instruction, study programmes or tuition where more than fifty students are involved, and expressly exclude counselling, sports, dance, theatre and other creative activities. Most schools and serious coaching centres fall inside that scope.

When does the admission season actually start for coaching institutes?

Earlier than agencies plan for. Parents shortlist roughly six weeks before enrolment, so April to June batches are decided in February and March, and October to November batches in late August. Board and entrance results are peak-attention moments, not the start of the decision.
