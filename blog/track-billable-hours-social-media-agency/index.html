How to Track Billable Hours in a Social Media Agency (Even on Retainers)

By Nishant Singh, founder of My Digital Sevak — written after running a social media agency across 63+ brands · Published 22 Nov 2026

To track billable hours in a social media agency, log time on the task itself, not in a separate sheet. Use 8–10 fixed categories and mark each entry billable or non-billable. Have a manager approve the week. Then compare each client's hours with their retainer, and invoice out-of-scope hours as a separate GST line.

For years we didn't track time at all. Every client was on a monthly retainer, so the fee was fixed and the hours felt irrelevant. Then we finally looked at two clients on the same ₹25,000 package. One took roughly a day a week of team time. The other took close to three, because every post went through four rounds of changes on WhatsApp. Same invoice, completely different business.

That's the real reason a social media agency tracks hours. It's less about billing by the hour and more about knowing what each retainer actually costs you. The billing part, for genuinely extra work, comes as a bonus.

What counts as billable in a social media agency?

Billable means work done for a specific client that the client is paying for, under the retainer or as extra. Everything that runs the agency itself is non-billable. Everhour's guide for social media managers draws the line the same way: strategy, content creation, scheduling, community management and reporting are billable. Sales calls, internal admin and training are not, unless the contract says otherwise. Here's the category list we'd start with:

Two grey areas cause most arguments. Revisions: rounds within your agreed limit are billable time against that client but covered by the retainer. Rounds beyond the limit are extra work, if your retainer agreement says so. Redo caused by your own mistake (a wrong logo, a typo) is time spent on the client, but you shouldn't charge for it. Log it as non-billable so it still shows up when you check what that client costs.

Why track hours if every client is on a fixed retainer?

Because it turns "this client feels heavy" into a number. Divide the monthly retainer by the hours logged on that client and you get the effective hourly rate. Compare it with what an hour of your team actually costs (salaries plus overheads, divided by working hours) and you can see which clients make money and which ones you're subsidising. Our guide to social media agency profit margins walks through that maths.

It also changes the renewal conversation. "We'd like to increase the fee" is a negotiation. "Your account took 61 hours last month against the 35 we scoped, and here's where they went" is a fact the client can check.

How to set up time tracking without your team hating it

  1. Fix the categories first. Eight to ten, like the table above. Twenty categories means people pick at random.
  2. Track on the task. The reel, the carousel, the shoot. If time lives in a separate sheet, it gets filled in on Friday from memory, and memory always rounds down.
  3. Use a timer for long work, manual entry for short work. Nobody should start a timer for a two-minute caption fix. End-of-day entry is fine for that.
  4. Log daily, approve weekly. A manager reviews each person's week on Monday. They look for missing days and odd entries, not for minutes.
  5. Set rates once. A default hourly rate, plus overrides per person or per client where needed.
  6. Review monthly, per client. Hours against retainer, billable against non-billable, and the out-of-scope hours you can actually invoice.

And say out loud why you're doing it. If the team thinks timesheets exist to catch slow people, the numbers will be fiction within a month. In our office the line is: "This is so we can price clients properly and push back when they want more."

What billable utilisation should you aim for?

Utilisation is billable hours divided by total hours worked. The time-tracking vendors give similar targets. Clockify says most marketing agencies aim for 70–80%. Harvest suggests around 70–75% for individuals and about 60% for the agency as a whole. Both also warn that pushing it too high burns people out.

In a small Indian agency it splits by role. Designers and video editors can sit near the top of that range, because nearly all their work is client work. Account managers and founders sit much lower, since they carry sales, hiring and admin. If a maker is consistently above 85%, don't celebrate. They have no slack for the festival rush, and they're the next resignation.

How do you turn hours into a GST invoice?

Only bill hours as hours when the work is outside the retainer: an extra shoot, a landing page, a campaign the client asked for mid-month. Put it on the invoice as its own line, with the hours and rate visible:

Frequently asked questions

Should a social media agency track hours if clients pay a fixed retainer?

Yes. The retainer tells you what a client pays, not what they cost. Tracking hours shows which retainers are profitable, gives you evidence when the scope quietly grows, and lets you bill genuinely extra work, such as an unplanned shoot or a website job, by the hour instead of absorbing it.

Is client revision time billable?

Revisions within the rounds your contract includes are part of the retainer, so log them as billable time against that client but don't invoice them separately. Rounds beyond the agreed number, or changes to an already approved post, are extra work you can bill if the agreement says so.

What is a good billable utilisation rate for an agency?

Time-tracking vendors usually quote 70 to 80 percent for individual makers and around 60 percent for the agency as a whole, since founders and account managers carry sales and admin. Treat that as a direction, not a target to squeeze; staying above 85 percent for long tends to burn people out.
