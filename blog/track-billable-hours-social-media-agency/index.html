How to Track Billable Hours in a Social Media Agency

By Nishant Singh, founder of My Digital Sevak — written after running a social media agency across 63+ brands · Published October 21, 2026

Log every hour against a client and a task category such as design, video, copy, reporting, or calls and approvals. Mark it billable or non-billable, collect weekly timesheets, and review them monthly. On retainers, the hours reveal each client's real hourly rate. For out-of-scope work, approved hours go straight onto the GST invoice.

Almost no Indian social media agency bills by the hour. We charge a monthly retainer for 12 posts, 4 reels and a report, and the client never asks how long any of it took. So when someone says "track your hours", most founders shrug. Why measure something nobody pays for?

Because the retainer hides what each client costs you. Two clients can each pay ₹40,000 a month and take very different amounts of your team's time. One approves on the first round. The other sends voice notes at 11 PM, wants three versions of every reel and changes the festival plan twice a week. You only see the difference once the hours are written down, and by then you've usually been under-charging that second client for months.

Here's the system we use. The categories are what a social media team actually does, the billable line is drawn the same way every time, and the hours end up on a GST invoice whenever the work falls outside scope.

Why track hours if you bill on retainer?

Because hours are your cost. Divide each client's retainer by the hours your team logged against them and you get an effective hourly rate:

Effective hourly rate = monthly retainer ÷ hours logged for that client

As an illustration: a ₹40,000 retainer that took 62 hours works out to about ₹645 an hour. Now compare that with your cost per billable hour, which is salaries plus rent, software and your own time, divided by the billable hours your team actually logs. If that comes to ₹450, the client is profitable but thin. If a second client on the same fee took 110 hours, you earned about ₹364 an hour from them and lost money on every hour. Nothing else in the business shows you this as clearly.

Three decisions get much easier once you have this number:

  • Re-pricing at renewal. "Your account took 40% more time than we scoped" is a conversation you can have with a client. "We feel stretched" isn't.
  • Hiring. If the designers' logged hours have been close to full capacity for three months, it's time to hire. Our guide on how many clients one social media manager can handle uses the same logic.
  • Firing. Sometimes the hours show that a client will never be profitable at any price they'd accept.

What task categories should a social media agency track?

Keep it to eight to ten categories. With thirty, people pick at random and the data is worthless. Social media teams usually split work into strategy, content production, community management, analytics and reporting, because each one is billed differently (Everhour). This is the list we use:

The category most agencies forget is client calls & approvals. Chasing a "looks good" on WhatsApp feels like nothing, but across a month it often turns out to be the heaviest category for the most demanding client. If you only remember one row, remember that one.

Where does billable end and non-billable begin?

The cleanest rule we've found: if the client caused it or asked for it, it's billable. If we caused it, it isn't. Billable doesn't always mean it goes on an invoice. Most billable hours are already paid for by the retainer, and you log them so you can see what that client costs.

  • Revisions inside the agreed rounds (say, two per post) are billable and covered by the retainer.
  • A third round, or a new brief after approval, is out-of-scope work. You can only invoice it if the rate was agreed in advance, which is what an add-on price list is for. See also how to handle client revisions.
  • Rework from our own mistake is non-billable, and you should log it anyway. A designer with ten hours of internal rework a month needs a better brief or a checklist, not a scolding.
  • Pitching and proposals are non-billable, even for a client you then win. That time is a sales cost.

Price non-billable time into your rates rather than pretending it doesn't exist. Cloud Campaign's guide on billable vs non-billable hours makes the same point: admin, proposals and coordination have to be paid for by your billable rate, or by nobody.

What's a realistic billable utilisation rate?

Utilisation is billable hours divided by available hours. The published numbers mostly come from software vendors and consulting surveys, so use them as rough guides:

  • Harvest's agency calculator says most agencies run at 55–60% and puts a realistic target for service businesses at 70–80%. It also notes creative agencies may find 60–70% more realistic (Harvest).
  • For professional services overall, SPI Research's 2025 benchmark of 509 firms recorded average billable utilisation of 66.4%, the lowest in the study's 19-year history, down from about 75% a decade earlier (as reported by Beancount).

Roles matter more than the average. In our experience, designers and editors can sit at 70–85%, account managers lower, and the founder well under half, because pitching, hiring and invoicing all land on them. Our agency profit margin guide uses a 50–60% team average for the same reason. If your whole team reports 95%, people are logging time they didn't spend, or they're burning out.

How do you set up time tracking without the team hating it?

  1. Log time against tasks, not a blank timer. If every post, reel and report is already a task with a client attached, a timer on the task fills in the client and category for you. Typing "Client X – design – 1h 20m" into a spreadsheet at 7 PM is how tracking dies.
  2. Allow manual entries. Shoot days and client calls happen away from a laptop. Let people add "4h shoot" afterwards rather than insisting on a live timer.
  3. Weekly timesheets, approved by a manager. Each person submits their week on Friday. A manager approves or sends it back on Monday. Approved hours are the only ones that reach reports or invoices.
  4. Set rates once. Give each person an internal cost rate, and set a billable rate per client where you charge out-of-scope work. A client-specific rate should override the person's default.
  5. Review monthly, not daily. A five-minute look at effective rate per client once a month is enough. Micromanaging timers is how you lose good designers.
  6. Explain why. Tell the team it's for pricing clients, not for watching them. Then show them, the first time a client's fee goes up because of what the timesheets showed.

How do you turn tracked hours into a GST invoice?

For retainer work, the hours stay internal. The invoice shows the retainer, and the timesheet tells you whether you priced it well. For out-of-scope work you bill hourly, put the approved hours on their own line. A worked example for an intra-state client:

LineQtyRateAmount
Social media management retainer, October (SAC 998361)1₹40,000₹40,000
Extra reel editing beyond agreed scope, approved hours (SAC 998361)6.5 h₹1,200/h₹7,800
Taxable value₹47,800
CGST 9% + SGST 9%₹4,302 + ₹4,302
Total₹56,404

Three details keep these lines from causing arguments:

  • Round the hours first, then multiply. Bill 6.5 hours, not 6 hours 28 minutes at a per-minute rate, so that quantity × rate always equals the amount on the invoice.
  • Attach the approved timesheet for the out-of-scope line, with dates and what was done. A client who can see the entries rarely disputes the line.
  • Charge IGST instead if the client is in another state. Our GST invoice guide for digital marketing services covers SAC 998361, every mandatory field and when IGST applies.

Five mistakes that make the numbers useless

  • Only tracking billable time. Without the non-billable hours you can't calculate utilisation or your real cost per hour.
  • Letting people fill timesheets from memory at month-end. You get round numbers and fiction. Weekly is the minimum.
  • Invoicing unapproved hours. If a manager hasn't checked the entries, don't put them in front of a client.
  • Hiding scope creep inside the retainer. If out-of-scope hours keep appearing, the fix is a new scope at renewal, not more unpaid hours. Our retainer vs project pricing guide covers how to re-scope.
  • Never acting on the data. Tracking is worth the effort only if one client's price, scope or team changes because of it every quarter.

Frequently asked questions

Should a social media agency on monthly retainers track time at all?

Yes. The client never sees the hours, but you need them. Dividing each retainer by the hours logged against that client gives you an effective hourly rate, and that number shows which clients are profitable, which ones are quietly eating your team's week, and when it is time to re-price or re-scope.

What is a good billable utilisation rate for a small agency?

Vendor benchmarks put most agencies at 55 to 60 percent, with 70 to 80 percent a common target for producers such as designers and editors. Account managers and founders sit much lower because calls, pitching and admin are real work no client pays for. Aim for a team average in the 60s first.

Are revisions billable time?

Revisions within the rounds your retainer includes are billable work covered by the retainer, so log them against the client. Rework caused by your own mistake is non-billable. Extra rounds beyond the agreed limit, or a change of brief after approval, are billable as an add-on, but only if the client agreed that rate in advance.

How do I bill tracked hours on a GST invoice?

Put the hours on their own line, for example 6.5 hours of extra reel editing at 1,200 rupees an hour, under SAC 998361, then add 18 percent GST as CGST plus SGST or as IGST depending on the client's state. Round hours before multiplying, and attach the approved timesheet so the client can check every entry.
