LUT Export of Services Invoice: A Guide for Indian Agencies

By Nishant Singh, founder of My Digital Sevak — written after running a social media agency across 63+ brands · Published 26 Nov 2026

To bill a foreign client at 0% GST, an Indian agency files a Letter of Undertaking (LUT) in Form GST RFD-11 once every financial year. Each export invoice then carries no IGST, states that the supply is under an LUT, and must be paid in foreign exchange within one year. All five export conditions in Section 2(6) of the IGST Act must be met.

The first foreign client usually arrives by accident. A restaurant owner you work with in Haldwani has a cousin running a café in Dubai. A UK founder finds your reels on Instagram. A US agency wants a white-label team in India for its carousels. The work is the same as for your Indian clients. The invoice is not.

Most small agencies get one of two things wrong. Some add 18% GST to the bill, and the foreign client, who can't claim Indian GST back, asks why they're paying an extra 18%. Others drop GST without filing anything and find out at their first GST notice that "zero-rated" doesn't mean "no paperwork". The LUT sits between those two mistakes. Here is how it works for an agency, in the order you'll actually do it.

What is an LUT, and why does an agency need one?

Exports of services are zero-rated under Section 16 of the IGST Act. You can make a zero-rated supply in one of two ways:

  1. Under an LUT, without paying IGST. You file a Letter of Undertaking once a year, and every export invoice in that year goes out at 0%.
  2. With IGST paid, and a refund later. You charge and pay IGST on each invoice, then apply for a refund of that tax.

For a small agency, the second route is a cash-flow trap. On a ₹1,00,000-a-month retainer, that's ₹18,000 of your money sitting with the government every month until the refund is processed. The LUT costs nothing to file and avoids all of it. The only people who can't use it, according to CalcGuru's export-of-services guide, are those prosecuted for tax evasion of ₹2.5 crore or more, who furnish a bond instead.

Is your foreign client really an export client?

This is the step most agencies skip. A supply counts as an export of services only when all five conditions in Section 2(6) of the IGST Act are met. Four out of five makes it a normal domestic supply with GST.

#ConditionWhat it means for an agency
1The supplier is located in IndiaYour registered agency. Easy.
2The recipient is located outside IndiaYou bill the foreign company itself, not its Indian distributor, franchisee or subsidiary.
3The place of supply is outside IndiaFor ordinary marketing services, this follows where the recipient is located.
4Payment is received in convertible foreign exchange (or in rupees where the RBI permits)Dollars, dirhams or pounds through a bank or remittance service. A rupee transfer from someone's Indian account doesn't qualify.
5Supplier and recipient are not merely establishments of the same personBilling your own branch or office abroad doesn't count.

The cases we see go wrong are almost always condition 2 or 4. A Dubai brand asks you to bill their Delhi office "because it's easier". That's a domestic invoice with GST. Or the client is abroad, but their brother in Mumbai pays you by UPI. That fails the foreign-exchange condition, and the zero rating with it.

One recent change is worth knowing about. Indian agencies doing white-label work for foreign agencies used to worry about being treated as an "intermediary", whose place of supply was India. CalcGuru and TaxClue both report that the Finance Act, 2026 omitted that intermediary rule, Section 13(8)(b), from 30 March 2026. If your foreign work involves arranging or referring business rather than doing it yourself, confirm your position with your CA. For a studio that makes the content itself, this was rarely the issue anyway.

How do you file an LUT on the GST portal?

It takes about fifteen minutes and costs nothing:

  1. Log in to the GST portal and open Services → User Services → Furnish Letter of Undertaking (LUT).
  2. Choose the financial year the LUT is for.
  3. Fill in Form GST RFD-11, including the two independent witnesses it asks for.
  4. Sign it with your DSC or EVC and submit. Save the acknowledgement (ARN) PDF.

An LUT is valid for one financial year. Make it a habit to file next year's in March, before the first invoice of April. If a foreign client turns up mid-year and you have no LUT yet, file it before you raise that first zero-rated invoice, and talk to your CA about anything already billed.

What goes on a zero-rated export invoice?

Everything a normal tax invoice has (see our GST invoice format for digital marketing), plus these:

  • The Rule 46 endorsement, word for word: "SUPPLY MEANT FOR EXPORT/SUPPLY TO SEZ UNIT OR SEZ DEVELOPER FOR AUTHORISED OPERATIONS UNDER BOND OR LETTER OF UNDERTAKING WITHOUT PAYMENT OF INTEGRATED TAX".
  • The client's full foreign address and the country, where you'd normally write an Indian state as place of supply.
  • IGST at 0%, with the same SAC code you'd use for an Indian client.
  • Your LUT reference. Not strictly required by the rule, but many CAs ask for the ARN on the invoice because it ties the invoice to the LUT at a glance.
  • The agreed foreign-currency amount, if you've priced in dollars or dirhams, and the exchange rate you used for the rupee value.

Then report the invoice in Table 6A (Exports) of GSTR-1 as an export without payment of tax, not in the B2B table. If your turnover is over the e-invoicing threshold, export invoices still need an IRN; our e-invoicing guide for agencies explains when that applies.

The one-year payment rule nobody tells you about

Zero rating isn't final when you raise the invoice. Under Rule 96A, the money for an exported service has to arrive within one year of the invoice date. If it doesn't, you pay the IGST you didn't charge, with 18% interest, within 15 days.

Foreign clients are no better at paying on time than Indian ones. Their finance teams sit in a different time zone and don't feel your follow-ups the way a local client does. Three habits help:

  • Take the first month in advance. It's standard practice with international retainers and nobody serious objects.
  • Keep the bank's remittance advice (FIRC or FIRA) for every receipt, filed against the invoice number. That's your proof the money arrived in foreign exchange.
  • Treat a 60-day overdue export invoice as urgent, not "they're abroad, it'll come". Our guide on handling late-paying clients has the follow-up sequence we use.
The LUT saves you the 18% up front. The one-year rule is the price: if a foreign invoice goes unpaid, the GST comes back to you with interest.

Don't forget your input credit

Because you collect no GST on exports, the GST you pay on Canva, Adobe, Meta Verified, laptops and your CA's fee can pile up as unused credit if foreign work is a large share of your billing. Under an LUT, you can claim a refund of that accumulated credit. If exports become a big slice of your revenue, ask your CA about it; our input credit guide for agencies covers what you can claim in the first place.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to file an LUT every year?

Yes. A Letter of Undertaking is valid for one financial year, so you file a fresh one in Form GST RFD-11 for each year, ideally in March before the new year starts. Filing is online and free. Without a valid LUT, an export invoice has to carry IGST that you later claim back as a refund.

What happens if the foreign client pays late?

Under Rule 96A, payment for an exported service must come in within one year of the invoice date. If it doesn't, you pay the IGST you didn't charge, with 18% interest, within 15 days. So chase overseas invoices as seriously as Indian ones, and keep the bank's remittance advice for each receipt.

Is a client in India with a foreign parent company an export client?

No. If you bill the Indian company, the recipient is located in India and the supply is domestic, so normal GST applies even if the money comes from the parent abroad. Export treatment needs the recipient, the place of supply and the payment all to be outside India, along with the other two conditions.
