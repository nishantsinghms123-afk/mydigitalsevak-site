Instagram Trial Reels for Brands: How Agencies Should Use Them

By Nishant Singh, founder of My Digital Sevak — written after running a social media agency across 63+ brands · Published October 3, 2026

Instagram Trial Reels let a professional account show a Reel only to people who don't follow it. It stays off the grid and out of followers' feeds; after about 24 hours you see views, likes, comments and shares, then share it with followers yourself or let Instagram do it if views are strong in the first 72 hours.

Every agency has had this conversation. The client wants to try something new on Reels — a meme format, a founder talking to camera, Hinglish instead of polished English — but is scared it will look odd next to the rest of the grid. So the idea dies in the WhatsApp group, and the account keeps posting the same product-on-a-table Reel it posted last quarter.

Trial Reels solve exactly that fear. But most guides about them are written for solo creators testing their own content. A brand account run by an agency has extra problems: someone has to approve the Reel, someone has to decide whether it lands on the client's grid, and someone has to explain the numbers in next month's report. This post is about those problems.

What are Instagram Trial Reels, exactly?

Instagram announced Trial Reels on December 10, 2024, after testing them since May 2024. Here is how the feature behaves, per Meta's own announcement and Instagram's creators blog:

  • Who can use it: professional accounts — Business or Creator. Personal accounts can't.
  • How you post one: make a Reel as usual and, before sharing, turn on the "Trial" toggle. The Reel then sits in your profile alongside drafts.
  • Who sees it: non-followers, through Reels and other recommendation surfaces. It doesn't appear on the main grid or Reels tab, or in followers' feeds.
  • The catch: followers can still reach it if someone sends it to them in a DM, or via audio, location or effect pages.
  • Metrics: after roughly 24 hours, you see views, likes, comments and shares.
  • Graduating it: either tap to share it with everyone, or switch on automatic sharing, which Instagram applies "if we determine it's performing well based on the views it receives within the first 72 hours." You can change that setting later.

Two later changes matter for agencies. In April 2026, Adam Mosseri said Trial Reels can now be scheduled ahead of time in the app. And since December 2025, Meta's Content Publishing API accepts a trial_params setting with two graduation options: MANUAL (you graduate it in the app) and SS_PERFORMANCE (Instagram graduates it automatically if it performs well), according to the platform changelog. So some publishing tools can now post trial versions too.

Does it work? The only numbers Instagram has shared, reported by Social Media Today in June 2025: after trying the feature, 40% of creators started posting Reels more often, and of those, 80% saw higher Reels reach from non-followers. Those are creator figures, not brand figures. Read them as "people who experiment more get found more," not as a promise for your client.

Why should brands care if they aren't creators?

Because the problem it solves is bigger for brands. A creator's audience forgives a weird post. A brand's audience includes customers, distributors, the founder's relatives and, in our experience, at least one competitor watching everything. That's why brand accounts get conservative, and conservative Reels stop reaching new people.

A Trial Reel gives you a place to be less conservative without spending the grid on it. For the brands we manage, the useful questions it answers are:

  • Will strangers watch this brand at all in a new format — a skit, a street interview, a behind-the-scenes clip?
  • Does a Hindi or regional-language hook pull better than English for this product?
  • Does putting the ₹ price in the first second help or hurt?
  • Does the founder on camera beat a voiceover over product shots?

None of those is a question you want answered on the main grid with a flop that sits there for years.

"Trial" doesn't mean private: the approval rule

This is the mistake I expect agencies to make. Because a Trial Reel is off the grid, it feels like a draft. It isn't. It's published, under the client's handle, to people who have never heard of the brand. For many of them it's their first impression.

So every Trial Reel goes through the same approval as a normal post. Same brand-safety check, same claims check, same music rules — the licensing questions in our guide to copyright music on Reels for Indian brands don't go away because the audience is smaller. If the Reel features an influencer or a paid collaboration, the disclosure rules in our ASCI influencer guidelines piece apply in full.

What we change is the approval framing. Clients approve the test, not just the asset: "We want to see if a Hinglish hook beats your usual English one. Here are both versions. Neither goes on your grid unless you say so." Clients who would never approve an experiment on the grid say yes to that far more readily. If you need a structure for it, slot trial versions into the review stage of your content approval workflow with a label so nobody mistakes them for grid posts.

Should you let Instagram auto-share a brand's Trial Reel?

For brand accounts, our default is manual. Automatic sharing means Instagram, based on views, decides what goes on your client's grid and into their followers' feeds. A Reel can earn lots of stranger views for reasons the brand won't like — a controversial joke, a price that's since changed, a festive offer that ended two days ago.

Manual graduation adds one step: you look at the 24-hour numbers, send the winner to the client with one line of context, and share it on their go-ahead. That takes a minute and keeps the agency from explaining an auto-shared Reel on a Monday call. The one case where we'd consider automatic sharing is a time-bound push — say, a Diwali sale Reel — where the content has already been approved for the grid and you only want Instagram to choose the strongest of several cuts.

A testing plan that gives clean answers

Trial Reels are only useful if you can read the result. Most "tests" we see change five things at once and prove nothing. The method we use:

  1. Change one thing. Same footage, different hook. Or same script, different language. Never a new format, new music and new caption together.
  2. Compare trials with trials. A Trial Reel is shown to non-followers only, while a normal Reel reaches followers first, so the numbers aren't directly comparable. Keep a log of trials per client and compare inside that log.
  3. Post at the same time each week. Now that trials can be scheduled, keep timing fixed so it isn't the variable. Our IST posting windows by day are a reasonable starting point.
  4. Read shares and comments, not just views. Views say the hook worked. Shares say the idea worked. For brands, the second matters more.
  5. Decide within 72 hours. Graduate it, archive it, or note the lesson. A Trial Reel left in limbo teaches no one anything.

Instagram allows up to 20 Trial Reels a day, according to the same Social Media Today report. No brand needs that. Two or three a week, planned like any other deliverable, is plenty for most clients.

Clients care less about the trials than about what you learned from them. One short section in the monthly social media report is enough:

Where Trial Reels don't help

  • Accounts that rely on followers. A local clinic or coaching institute in a city like Lucknow or Indore often gets its leads from people who already follow it. Stranger reach in another state isn't the goal.
  • Anything time-critical. A same-day offer can't wait 24 hours for a test. Post it normally.
  • Carousels and Stories. Trials are for Reels only; Meta's API documentation describes them as Reels shared to non-followers.
  • Weak ideas. A trial tells you whether strangers like a Reel. It won't fix a brand with nothing interesting to say.

Frequently asked questions

Can a business account use Instagram Trial Reels?

Yes. Instagram made Trial Reels available to professional accounts, which covers both Business and Creator accounts. Personal accounts cannot use the feature. If a brand's professional account still does not show the Trial toggle when sharing a Reel, it is not eligible yet; check Instagram's Help Center for the current requirements rather than guessing.

Do followers ever see a Trial Reel?

Not in their Feed or Reels tab, and it does not appear on the profile grid while it is a trial. But Instagram says followers can still come across it if someone sends it to them in a DM, or through audio, location or effect pages. Treat a Trial Reel as public content, because it is.

Should agencies turn on automatic sharing for client Trial Reels?

For most brand accounts, no. Automatic sharing lets Instagram move the Reel onto the grid and into followers' feeds if it performs well on views in the first 72 hours. That decision changes the brand's profile, so it should go through the client. Use manual sharing and graduate winners after a quick review.

How many Trial Reels can a brand post?

Instagram limits accounts to 20 Trial Reels per day, according to Social Media Today's coverage of Instagram's guidance. No brand needs anywhere near that. Two or three trials a week, each testing a single change against the brand's usual format, gives an agency cleaner answers than a burst of uploads it cannot properly compare.
