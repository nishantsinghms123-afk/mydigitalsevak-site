Social Media Marketing for Ayurveda & Wellness Brands in India

By Nishant Singh, founder of My Digital Sevak — written after running a social media agency across 63+ brands · Published October 11, 2026

Ayurveda brands win on social media by selling trust, not cures. Tell sourcing and process stories, put a qualified vaidya on camera, and plan around seasons and festivals. Never claim to treat a disease in the DMR Act's schedule (diabetes, blood pressure, obesity and the rest), and treat every reshared testimonial and influencer post as the brand's own ad.

An Ayurveda or wellness client looks like an easy account. The products photograph well, there's a centuries-old story behind every ingredient, and customers who love the brand will happily post about it. The difficulty is that the claims customers most want to hear, "this fixed my sugar" or "my BP is normal now", are exactly the ones the law says the brand cannot make.

That gap is where Ayurveda accounts go wrong. In 2023–24, healthcare was the most violative sector in ASCI's annual complaints report, at 19% of cases. Of the 1,575 healthcare ads ASCI processed, 1,249 violated the Drugs and Magic Remedies Act and were reported to the Ministry of AYUSH, and 86% of the healthcare ads appeared on digital platforms. Every one of those started as somebody's caption, and plenty of them were written by an agency.

This is the playbook we'd hand an account manager taking on an Ayurvedic, herbal or wellness client.

Which laws govern Ayurveda advertising on social media?

The Drugs and Magic Remedies Act, 1954

This is the one that matters most. Section 3(d) bars advertising any drug for the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of the diseases and conditions listed in its schedule. The list includes diabetes, heart diseases, high or low blood pressure and obesity, among others. It is the law the Supreme Court used against Patanjali in the Indian Medical Association's case, which ended in public apologies from the company and its managing director. The Act doesn't distinguish between a newspaper ad and an Instagram caption.

Rule 170, and what changed in 2025

Rule 170 of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945 used to require approval from the state licensing authority before an Ayurvedic, Siddha or Unani drug ad could run. The Ministry of AYUSH omitted it in July 2024. The Supreme Court stayed that omission in August 2024, then vacated the stay in August 2025 when it closed the IMA's petition. So there is no state pre-approval step any more.

Don't read that as "anything goes". Pre-approval was a gate before publishing. Its removal moves the whole burden onto the brand and its agency, and the DMR Act, the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 and the ASCI code all still apply.

Check which licence the product sits under

Before writing a single caption, ask the client for the licence on the label. An Ayurvedic medicine is licensed under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act. A herbal powder or health drink may be licensed by FSSAI as a food or nutraceutical, with its own claims rules. A face oil may be a cosmetic. Each one allows different claims, and the brand's regulatory or quality person should confirm which applies to each product.

Comment replies are the leak nobody plans for. A customer asks "will this help my thyroid?" and a junior community manager types "yes, many customers have seen results". That reply is the brand making a claim in public. Give the team a fixed reply such as "We can't advise on medical conditions here. Please speak to your doctor, or book a free consultation with our vaidya," and get it approved once.

What content actually works for Ayurveda brands?

The good news is that the content the law allows is also the content that sells. Ayurveda buyers are buying trust: who makes this, where the herbs come from, whether a real practitioner stands behind it.

  1. Origin and process reels. The farm, the drying racks, the copper vessels, the batch number being stamped. These are cheap to shoot and hard for a white-label competitor to copy.
  2. The vaidya on camera. A qualified BAMS practitioner explaining doshas, dinacharya (daily routine) or how to take a churna correctly builds more trust than any graphic. Keep it educational and away from product cure claims.
  3. Seasonal routines. Ayurveda already has a content calendar built in: ritucharya, the seasonal regimen. Monsoon digestion, winter skin, summer cooling drinks. Plan the year around these.
  4. Honest usage demos. How much, when, with what, and what it tastes like. Bitter is fine to admit; it makes everything else believable.
  5. Customer routines, not customer cures. A customer showing their morning kadha ritual is great UGC. The same customer saying it fixed their sugar is not usable.

For the festive calendar, fix these dates first: National Ayurveda Day, now fixed on 23 September (it used to move with Dhanteras), International Day of Yoga on 21 June, and Diwali gifting season, which is where most Ayurveda D2C brands make their quarter. How should Ayurveda brands work with wellness influencers?

Influencers are where most of the risk now sits, because a brief that says "talk about the benefits" turns into "this cured my PCOS" by the time the reel is posted. Three rules:

  • Disclosure on every paid post. The label has to be on the video or the image itself, not buried in hashtags. Our ASCI influencer guidelines guide sets out the formats.
  • Qualified voices for technical advice. Under ASCI's health rules, as narrowed in April 2025, an influencer who gives technical health information or advice must hold a relevant qualification and show it. Use a qualified practitioner for "how it works" content and lifestyle creators for "how I use it".
  • Write the banned claims into the contract. List the scheduled diseases the creator must not mention, require a draft before posting, and keep the right to ask for edits. Our influencer contract template has a clause you can adapt.

How should an agency run approvals for an Ayurveda client?

The fix is a process, not more caution from the writer. What we do on health-adjacent accounts:

  • A claims register. One shared document listing the approved phrases for each product, signed off by the client's regulatory person. Writers pick from it; anything new goes back for sign-off.
  • Named sign-off on every post. Not a thumbs-up on WhatsApp. If a complaint lands six months later, you need to show who approved what and when. A written content approval workflow makes that automatic.
  • A banned-words check before scheduling. Cure, reverse, permanent, guaranteed, the scheduled diseases by name. A two-minute search of each caption catches most problems.
  • Separate the clinic from the shop. If the brand also runs a consultation centre, its doctors are bound by their own professional rules. Our guide to social media for doctors and clinics covers that side.

A lean Ayurveda retainer we'd start with: 12 feed posts a month (four process or origin reels, three vaidya explainers, three routine or usage posts, two festive or seasonal), daily Stories, and a weekly comment-moderation sweep using the approved reply.

Frequently asked questions

Can an Ayurvedic brand say its product cures diabetes on Instagram?

No. Section 3(d) of the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 bars advertising any drug for the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of the diseases in its schedule, which includes diabetes, heart disease, blood pressure and obesity. An Instagram caption, reel or reshared testimonial counts as an advertisement.

Do Ayurvedic ads still need state licensing authority approval?

Not since August 2025. Rule 170, which required state approval for Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani drug ads, was omitted in July 2024, and the Supreme Court vacated its stay on that omission in August 2025. The DMR Act, the Consumer Protection Act and the ASCI code still apply in full.

Can an Ayurveda brand work with wellness influencers?

Yes, with conditions. Every paid post needs a clear disclosure label, and under ASCI's guidelines an influencer giving technical health advice must hold a relevant qualification and show it. Brief creators on how to use the product and how it feels, never on what disease it treats.

What content works best for Ayurveda brands on social media?

Ingredient sourcing stories, short explainers from a qualified vaidya, seasonal routines built on ritucharya, honest usage demos and customer routines that avoid disease claims. Ayurveda buyers want to know who makes the product and how it is made, so origin and process content usually outperforms polished product graphics.
